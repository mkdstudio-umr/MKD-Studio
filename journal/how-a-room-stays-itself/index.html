How a Room Stays Itself

Cipriani London opened on Davies Street in 1999. The dining room looks the way it looked then. The round tables. The white linen. The portrait above the banquette. Nothing has been updated because nothing needed updating. The room decided what it was and has been holding that position for twenty-five years.

This is rarer than it sounds. Most rooms accumulate revisions. A chair is replaced, then the chairs. The artwork is swapped for something more current. A renovation brings lighter tones and a shorter menu. Each individual decision seems reasonable, and the room becomes unrecognisable. The quiet rooms are the ones that found their argument early and trusted it.

The bookshop with the easel leaning against the Phaidon shelf has no explanation for the easel. It was probably there before anyone now working in the shop arrived. It will probably be there after they leave. It is part of the room now, and removing it would make the room less itself without anyone being able to say exactly why.

Staying itself is an active decision. Not renovating is a choice. Keeping the portrait at the same height, the linen at the same standard, the newspapers stacked on the same table: these require the same attention as choosing them in the first place. The room that stays itself is working constantly.

It is just not showing the work.
