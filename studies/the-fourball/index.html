The Fourball

Kent, United Kingdom / Sporting hospitality
Setting

A corporate golf day is not arranged around golfers. It is arranged around companies, and then the companies are divided into fours.

The sheet goes up in the morning. Four names, a tee time, a starting hole. From that point the day is settled: these four people, this course, the next five hours.

None of the four chose the other three.

Weather

It rained from the first tee until the turn.

Umbrellas went up on the path before anyone had reached a fairway. Bags were carried rather than wheeled across the wet ground, and the club crest on the ball washer sat beaded with water all morning.

Rain is the one part of a golf day that cannot be hosted. It arrives for everybody at once, and the four of them are wet together before they have finished exchanging names.

Pace

Five hours is a long time to spend with three people you have just met, and far too long to spend in silence.

Golf leaves gaps. The walk to the next shot. The wait on a blind tee. The minute spent standing still while somebody else reads a putt.

Conversation fills them because there is nothing else available to fill them with. Very little of it is about golf.

Recognition

By the back nine the group has a shape. Who hits first. Who talks. Who turns out to be good.

A phone comes out on the fairway and somebody is photographed mid-swing by a man he met at half past seven.

At the flag they stand together for a picture, the way groups do at the end of a round.

After

Trolleys return to the clubhouse four at a time.

Bags are stacked in the corridor, shoes are changed, hands are shaken in a room with a bell on the wall.

The names on the sheet are no longer a draw. The next conversation between them will be about work.

Notes on the Study

The day was the McLaren Construction Group Golf Day, held on 17 September 2026 at Rochester & Cobham Park in Kent and sponsored by Glaze Aluminium Facades, which brought five of its own suppliers with it. Construction is an industry of long-distance relationships: a facade is drawn in one place, extruded in another, glazed in a third, and fitted on site by people who arrive after the glass has gone in. A golf day is one of the few occasions on which those people stand next to each other.

The photographs follow the groups rather than the golf. Almost nothing here is a decisive sporting moment; the shots that mattered were the ones between shots, when four people assigned to one another by a sheet on a clubhouse wall worked out how to spend the morning. The posed frame at each flag repeats through the day, group after group, which is as close as the archive comes to a record of who met whom.

It pairs directly with The Ritual, a golf day in Leeds photographed almost entirely without faces, where the subject was the objects a round leaves behind. The two studies take opposite halves of the same occasion. The full day in Kent is published as Common Ground, one hundred and eleven photographs across nine chapters.
